How the mBot works

The mBot is a small two-wheeled robot with one circuit board for a brain. This page explains what each part does, how the robot decides what to do, and how your computer talks to it.

Sense, think, act

Every robot, from a vacuum cleaner to a Mars rover, repeats the same loop. It senses the world, thinks about what it found, then acts. The mBot runs this loop hundreds of times a second. If the distance sensor reports a wall 8 cm ahead, the program decides to turn, and the motors carry that out before the next reading arrives.

The senses

ULTRASONIC
port 3

Distance sensor: the two “eyes”

The two round cylinders on the front look like eyes, but they work like a bat’s ears. One sends out a click of sound too high for people to hear. The other listens for the echo. The longer the echo takes, the farther away the object is.

Sound travels about 343 metres per second, so it covers 1 cm in about 29 microseconds. The sound has to go out and come back, so the board divides by two:

distance (cm) = echo time (µs) ÷ 58

A wall 10 cm away sends the echo back in about 580 microseconds. The sensor works from about 3 cm to 400 cm, and reports 400 when nothing is in range. Soft or slanted things like curtains and chair legs can absorb or deflect the sound, so it can miss them.

LINE FOLLOWER
port 2

Line follower: two eyes looking down

Under the front of the robot are two small infrared lights, each paired with a light detector. White paper bounces infrared light back. Black tape soaks it up. Each eye only answers one question: “do I see black?” The board combines the two answers into a single number from 0 to 3.

If you lift the robot off the table, both eyes see nothing reflected, so they report black. That’s why our robot read 0.

LIGHT
on board

Light sensor

A small light-sensitive part on the top of the board turns brightness into a number from 0 (dark) to 1023 (very bright). You can use it to make the mBot wake up when the lights come on, or hide under a table.

BUTTON · IR
on board

Button and remote receiver

The button on the board is a simple on/off input. The factory program uses it to switch modes. The IR receiver picks up invisible infrared flashes from the remote control, the same way a TV does. Each remote key sends a different pattern of flashes.

Moving and steering

The mBot has no steering wheel. It steers the way a tank or a wheelchair does, by spinning its two wheels at different speeds. This is called differential drive. The small ball or skid at the front just slides along for balance.

The two motors are mounted facing opposite directions, so to drive forward the program actually spins one of them “backwards”. Speeds go from −255 (full reverse) to 255 (full forward).

Lights and sound

Two colour LEDs

Each LED holds a tiny red, green and blue light. Mixing them at different brightnesses (0 to 255 each) makes any colour. Red and green together make yellow. All three at full make white.

Buzzer

The buzzer is a tiny speaker that clicks back and forth very quickly. The number of clicks per second sets the note: 262 per second is middle C and 440 is the A above it. Programs can play beeps or whole tunes.

Out of the box: three modes

A new mBot already has a program inside it. Turn it on, then press the button on the board (or A, B or C on the remote) to switch modes. The LEDs change colour and the buzzer beeps so you know which mode you’re in.

A

Remote control

Drive with the arrow keys on the IR remote. The number keys set the speed.

B

Avoid obstacles

The robot drives on its own and turns away when the distance sensor sees something close.

C

Follow a line

Put it on the black track map from the box. It uses the line follower to stay on the line.

How your computer talks to it

By USB cable

A chip on the board (a CH340) turns USB into a simple serial connection. On Windows it shows up as a COM port. Ours is COM3. If no COM port appears, install the CH340 driver.

By Bluetooth

The Bluetooth module plugs into the board and carries the same messages without a cable. Pair it with a tablet or phone using the mBlock app, or with a computer using the Makeblock Bluetooth dongle. Keep the robot close to the dongle the first time they pair.

Two ways to run your program

Live mode. Your program runs on the computer, and the computer sends the robot short orders like “read the distance sensor” or “left motor to 150”. Changes take effect straight away, but the robot stops when the connection drops. This needs the factory firmware, which our robot has.

Upload mode. mBlock turns your blocks into Arduino code and copies it onto the chip. The robot then runs it on its own, with no cable. Uploading replaces the factory program. To get modes A, B and C back, use mBlock’s “Update firmware” or “Reset default program” option.

Power

The mBot runs on either the 3.7 V rechargeable lithium battery or 4 AA batteries in the battery box. The power switch is on the board. With the lithium battery fitted, plugging in the USB cable also charges it. The motors use the most power, so a weak battery shows up first as slow, wobbly driving.

When something seems wrong

  • The distance sensor always says 400. Nothing is within 4 metres, or the sensor cable isn’t clicked into port 3.
  • The line follower always says 0. The robot is lifted up, the cable isn’t in port 2, or the surface is too dark.
  • No COM port appears. Install the CH340 driver, then try a different USB cable. Some cables only carry power.
  • Live mode can’t connect after an upload. The uploaded program replaced the factory firmware. Use “Update firmware” in mBlock.
  • It drives in curves when it should go straight. Cheap motors never match exactly. Give the slower wheel a slightly higher speed.
